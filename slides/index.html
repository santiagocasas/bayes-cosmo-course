We have one Universe
Frequentist
  • Probability = long-run frequency of outcomes
  • Parameters are fixed, data are random
  • Needs repeatable experiments
  • No prior: parameters are not random, so they get no probability; earlier knowledge enters as extra data or fixed constraints
Bayesian
  • Probability = degree of belief
  • Data are fixed (observed); parameters carry a distribution
  • Works for a single realisation
  • Prior \( p(\boldsymbol{\theta}\,|\,M) \): what we knew before the data; it must be stated, and enough data overwhelm it
The question we actually want to answer
How probable are the parameters, given the data we have? \( p(\boldsymbol{\theta}\,|\,D) \)
Both camps use the same rules of probability; they differ in what is allowed to be a probability.
Bayes’ theorem
\textcolor{#1B5E8C}{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}\,|\,D,M)}=\frac{\textcolor{#C62828}{p(D\,|\,\boldsymbol{\theta},M)}\;\textcolor{#777777}{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}\,|\,M)}}{p(D\,|\,M)}
  • Posterior: what we know after seeing the data
  • Likelihood: forward model; the data we expect for given parameters
  • Prior: what we knew before (earlier experiments, physical limits)
  • Evidence: \( Z = p(D|M)=\int \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol\theta\,p(D|\boldsymbol\theta,M)\,p(\boldsymbol\theta|M) \)
For parameter estimation the evidence is only a normalisation; it becomes the main object when we compare models (Part III).
Bayes’ theorem is the product rule, read twice
Count the overlap \( A\cap B \) in two ways:
p(A,B) = p(A\,|\,B)\,p(B) = p(B\,|\,A)\,p(A)
\Rightarrow\quad p(A\,|\,B) = \frac{p(B\,|\,A)\,p(A)}{p(B)}
  • The theorem itself is one line of algebra.
  • The content is in using it for parameters: \( A\to\boldsymbol{\theta} \), \( B\to D \).
  • The hard parts are the likelihood, the prior and the integrals.

Priors are overwhelmed by data; updating is consistent
Two observers with Gaussian priors on H0: N(55, 12) (blue) and N(85, 12) (red). Same mock data: the 3, 10, 30 nearest galaxies. One parameter only: the offset a is fixed to 0 here. Each panel has its own y-scale.
\frac{1}{\sigma_N^2}=\frac{1}{\sigma_0^2}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{d_i^2}{\sigma^2},\qquad \mu_N=\sigma_N^2\Big(\frac{\mu_0}{\sigma_0^2}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{d_i\,cz_i}{\sigma^2}\Big)
  • The prior term \( 1/\sigma_0^2 \) is fixed; the data term grows with N, so both observers converge
  • The sums simply add: updating galaxy by galaxy (posterior of step 1 = prior of step 2) gives the same result as analysing all data jointly
  • If the prior still matters, the data are not informative enough
Gaussian likelihood and χ² (“chi-squared”)
L(\boldsymbol\theta)\propto\exp\Big[-\frac12\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\big(d_i-t_i(\boldsymbol\theta)\big)^2}{\sigma_i^2}\Big]
\Rightarrow\quad -2\ln L=\chi^2(\boldsymbol\theta)+\text{const},\qquad \chi^2=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\big(d_i-t_i(\boldsymbol\theta)\big)^2}{\sigma_i^2}
  • \(d_i\): measured data point (here: \(cz\) of galaxy \(i\))
  • \(t_i(\boldsymbol\theta)\): theory prediction for parameters \(\boldsymbol\theta\) (here: \(a+H_0 d_i\))
  • \(\sigma_i\): error of \(d_i\)
χ² (chi-squared): sum of squared, error-weighted residuals. Minimum χ² = best fit; \(\Delta\chi^2=\chi^2-\chi^2_{\min}\) sets the error bars.
A contour is a line of constant posterior; the region inside holds a fixed probability: 1σ = 68.3 %, 2σ = 95.4 % (Gaussian posterior).
Worked example: Hubble’s law with a nuisance offset
cz_i = a + H_0\,d_i + \epsilon_i
Gaussian noise \(\epsilon_i\) with \(\sigma = 300\) km/s (peculiar velocities).
  • Data: velocities \(cz_i\) at distances \(d_i\). As on the χ² slide: data \(cz_i\), theory \(t_i(\boldsymbol\theta)=a+H_0 d_i\) (here \(d_i\) is a distance)
  • Parameters \(\boldsymbol\theta=(H_0,a)\); \(\chi^2=\sum_i (cz_i-a-H_0 d_i)^2/\sigma^2\)
  • Nuisance parameter: a parameter the model needs, but we are not interested in its value. Here \(a\): a zero-point offset or bulk flow
  • Flat priors: \(H_0\in[40,100]\) km/s/Mpc, \(a\in[-500,500]\) km/s
  • \(N=30\) mock galaxies, \(d\in[20,400]\) Mpc; truth \(H_0=70\) km/s/Mpc, \(a=0\)
  • \(a\) and \(H_0\) are degenerate: the line pivots about the centre of the data, so a larger \(a\) needs a smaller \(H_0\)
Next: we analyse this with the Fisher matrix and with MCMC, and compare both with the exact grid posterior.
Fisher matrix and Hessian: the Gaussian approximation
Expand \(\ln L\) around its maximum \(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}\) (the gradient vanishes there):
\ln L(\boldsymbol\theta)\approx\ln L(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta})-\frac12\sum_{i,j}(\theta_i-\hat\theta_i)\,H_{ij}\,(\theta_j-\hat\theta_j)
H_{ij}=-\frac{\partial^2\ln L}{\partial\theta_i\,\partial\theta_j}\Big|_{\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}}
Hessian: the curvature of \(\ln L\) at the peak. Sharp peak = large curvature = small errors. With flat priors the posterior is a Gaussian with covariance \(H^{-1}\).
F_{ij}=\Big\langle-\frac{\partial^2\ln L}{\partial\theta_i\,\partial\theta_j}\Big\rangle=\sum_k\frac{1}{\sigma_k^2}\,\frac{\partial t_k}{\partial\theta_i}\,\frac{\partial t_k}{\partial\theta_j}
  • Fisher matrix \(F\): the Hessian averaged over data realisations. It needs only the model \(t(\boldsymbol\theta)\) and the errors, no data
  • Marginal error \(\sigma(\theta_i)=\sqrt{(F^{-1})_{ii}}\); conditional error \(1/\sqrt{F_{ii}}\) (all others fixed)
  • Our toy: \(\partial t_i/\partial a=1\), \(\partial t_i/\partial H_0=d_i\):
F=\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\begin{pmatrix}N&\sum_i d_i\\ \sum_i d_i&\sum_i d_i^2\end{pmatrix}
Linear model + Gaussian noise: Fisher is exact
\(t\) is linear in \(\boldsymbol\theta\), so \(\ln L\) is exactly quadratic: \(H\) depends neither on \(\boldsymbol\theta\) nor on the data, \(H=F\), and the posterior is exactly Gaussian (inside the flat priors).
Why? Differentiate \(\chi^2\) twice: exercise.
Next: sampling the posterior with MCMC; then grid, Fisher and MCMC live in the notebook.
Sampling: Metropolis–Hastings
  1. Start at \(\boldsymbol\theta_i\); compute \(p_i\propto\mathcal L\,\pi\)
  2. Propose \(\boldsymbol\theta_c\sim q(\boldsymbol\theta_c|\boldsymbol\theta_i)\)
  3. Accept with probability \(\min[1,p_c/p_i]\) (symmetric \(q\))
  4. If rejected, repeat \(\boldsymbol\theta_i\) in the chain
  5. Iterate; discard burn-in
Chain density → posterior; \(\langle f\rangle\approx\frac1N\sum_i f(\boldsymbol\theta_i)\).

Convergence: Gelman–Rubin \(\hat R\to 1\). Here (4 chains, burn-in 1000): \(\hat R=1.003\), acceptance about 22%.
Why this samples the posterior
  • Only the ratio \(p_c/p_i\) enters, so the unknown evidence cancels
  • The chain is a Markov process whose stationary distribution is the posterior
Cost scales roughly linearly with the number of parameters; a grid scales exponentially.
Metropolis–Hastings: the proposal width matters
Same posterior, same start (H0=45), 3000 steps each. Left: too small, the chain barely moves. Middle: well tuned. Right: too large, almost everything is rejected. A target acceptance of 20–30% is typical for a few parameters.
Always check: several chains from dispersed starts, \(\hat R\), autocorrelation, burn-in.

Recap of hour 1
  • Posterior = likelihood × prior / evidence: \( p(\boldsymbol\theta|D) \propto p(D|\boldsymbol\theta)\,p(\boldsymbol\theta) \)
  • Gaussian likelihood: \( -2\ln L=\chi^2 \); joint regions from \( \Delta\chi^2 \) (2.30 and 6.17 for two parameters)
  • Fisher matrix: quote the marginal error \( \sqrt{(F^{-1})_{ii}} \), not the conditional \( 1/\sqrt{F_{ii}} \)
  • Metropolis–Hastings: only posterior ratios enter, so the evidence cancels
  • Check convergence: several chains, \( \hat R\to 1 \), acceptance 20–30%, burn-in

Model selection: the evidence and Occam’s razor
B_{12}=\frac{Z_1}{Z_2}=\frac{\int\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol\theta_1\,p(D|\boldsymbol\theta_1,M_1)\,p(\boldsymbol\theta_1|M_1)}{\int\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol\theta_2\,p(D|\boldsymbol\theta_2,M_2)\,p(\boldsymbol\theta_2|M_2)}
  • \(M_1\): \(cz=H_0 d\) vs \(M_2\): \(cz=a+H_0 d\)
  • χ² never favours the simpler model; the evidence can, because extra prior volume is penalised
  • Jeffreys scale on \(|\ln B|\): below 1 inconclusive, 1–2.5 weak, 2.5–5 moderate, above 5 strong (Trotta)
  • Curves: mean ± 1σ over 200 mock catalogues of up to \(N=60\) galaxies
  • Truth \(a=0\) (\(M_1\) true): \(\ln B_{12}=1.0\pm0.8\) at \(N=60\), a mild Occam preference for the simpler \(M_1\)
  • Truth \(a=400\) km/s (\(M_2\) true): \(\ln B_{12}\approx-10\pm5\), strong preference for \(M_2\), growing with \(N\)
  • The result depends on the prior ranges (here \(a\in[-500,500]\))
Proposal geometry decides the efficiency
  • An isotropic step must be as small as the narrowest direction, so a correlated posterior is explored slowly along its long axis
  • For a Gaussian target in \(d\) dimensions the best random-walk step is about \(2.38/\sqrt d\) times the posterior width; acceptance then tends to 23%
  • Better: draw steps from the posterior covariance, tuned by an exploratory run (Padilla et al.) or taken from the Fisher matrix
  • Reparametrising to less correlated parameters helps just as much
\boldsymbol\theta_c=\boldsymbol\theta_i+\mathcal N\!\left(0,\;\tfrac{2.38^2}{d}\,\mathbf C\right),\qquad \mathbf C\approx F^{-1}
Gibbs sampling: one parameter at a time
  • Draw each parameter from its full conditional: \(\theta_1\sim p(\theta_1|\theta_2,\dots)\), then \(\theta_2\sim p(\theta_2|\theta_1,\dots)\)
  • Special case of Metropolis–Hastings in which every move is accepted; no tuning
  • Needs conditionals you can sample (conjugate priors, hierarchical models); in the Hubble toy both \(p(a|H_0)\) and \(p(H_0|a)\) are Gaussian
  • Strong correlations give the staircase path in the figure: many small steps
  • Used for CMB map-level inference and hierarchical models
Ensemble samplers: the stretch move
  • Many ‘walkers’ move together; each is updated using a partner chosen from the others
  • Affine invariance: a linear change of parameters, such as a correlated Gaussian, makes no difference, so no proposal covariance is needed
  • Rule of thumb: a few times \(d\) walkers; emcee is the standard code
  • Weak spots: very high dimension (tens of parameters) and multimodal posteriors
  • Needs only the log-posterior function
\boldsymbol\theta'=\mathbf y+z(\boldsymbol\theta-\mathbf y),\quad g(z)\propto z^{-1/2},\;\; z\in[\tfrac1a,a];\qquad p_\mathrm{acc}=\min\!\left[1,\,z^{d-1}\frac{p(\boldsymbol\theta')}{p(\boldsymbol\theta)}\right]
Hamiltonian Monte Carlo
  • Treat \(U=-\ln p\) as a potential energy, add a momentum \(\mathbf q\), and follow the Hamiltonian flow (leapfrog steps)
  • Long, informed jumps: high acceptance even in many dimensions
  • Costs: the gradient of ln p, and tuning of step size and trajectory length (the NUTS variant tunes the length automatically)
  • Gradients mean autodiff: cloelib is built on JAX, but a full Boltzmann-solver call is not differentiable; emulators are
  • Play with it: QR code, right
H(\boldsymbol\theta,\mathbf q)=U(\boldsymbol\theta)+\tfrac12\mathbf q^{T}M^{-1}\mathbf q,\qquad p_\mathrm{acc}=\min\!\left[1,\,e^{-\Delta H}\right]
Nested sampling: the evidence as a sum
  • Reorder the prior volume by likelihood: \(X(\lambda)\) is the prior mass with \(L>\lambda\), so the evidence is a 1D integral
  • \(N_\mathrm{live}\) points; at each step discard the worst and replace it by a new point with higher L; volume shrinks as \(X_i\approx e^{-i/N_\mathrm{live}}\)
  • Posterior samples come as a by-product (weights \(L_i\,\Delta X_i\))
  • Handles multimodal posteriors; MultiNest, dynesty, Nautilus
  • Run once for each model: this is how Bayes factors are computed
Z=\int L\,\mathrm{d}X\;\approx\;\sum_i L_i\,\Delta X_i

Practical rule. Use the simplest sampler that converges, check it on a case with a known answer (Hour 1), and compare with the Fisher forecast: when they disagree, learn why.
Fisher toolbox: combine, fix, marginalise
  • Combine independent probes: \(F=F_A+F_B\), parameters matched by name
  • Prior: a Gaussian prior of width \(\sigma_p\) adds \(1/\sigma_p^2\) to \(F_{ii}\)
  • Fix a parameter: delete its row and column (conditional errors)
  • Marginalise: invert \(F\), keep the sub-block; \(\sigma_i=\sqrt{(F^{-1})_{ii}}\)
  • Reparametrise: \(F_\phi=J^{T}F_\theta J\), \(J_{ij}=\partial\theta_i/\partial\phi_j\)
  • Figure of merit for \((w_0,w_a)\): \(\mathrm{FoM}=\sqrt{\det\tilde F_{w_0w_a}}\), \(\tilde F\) marginalised
In cosmicfishpie
  • fish_a + fish_b
  • marginalise(F, names)
  • reshuffle(F, names): others fixed
  • eliminate_parameters(F, names)
  • prior: add a 1×1 Fisher of 1/σ² for that parameter

Fisher matrix for large-scale structure
Photometric (WL, GCph, GGL): sum over multipoles, \(C_\ell\) is the matrix of all auxiliary tomographic spectra
F_{\alpha\beta}=\tfrac12 f_\mathrm{sky}\sum_\ell(2\ell+1)\,\mathrm{Tr}\!\left[C_\ell^{-1}\,\partial_\alpha C_\ell\,C_\ell^{-1}\,\partial_\beta C_\ell\right]
Spectroscopic (GCsp): sum over redshift bins \(i\), integral over \(k,\mu\); \(V_i\) is the bin volume
F_{\alpha\beta}=\sum_i\frac{1}{8\pi^2}\int\!\mathrm{d}\mu\int\!k^2\mathrm{d}k\;\partial_\alpha\ln P_\mathrm{obs}\,\partial_\beta\ln P_\mathrm{obs}\;V_i
Photometric ingredients
  • 10 equi-populated bins, z up to 2.5; 30 galaxies/arcmin²; shape noise 0.3
  • \(f_\mathrm{sky}=0.3636\); \(\ell_\mathrm{max}\) pessimistic 1500 (WL), 750 (GCph); optimistic 5000, 1500
  • Nuisance: 10 galaxy biases, 2 intrinsic-alignment amplitudes
Spectroscopic ingredients
  • 4 bins, 0.9 < z < 1.8; volumes 8–16 Gpc³/h³
  • \(k_\mathrm{max}\) = 0.25 (pess.), 0.30 (opt.) \(h\)/Mpc
  • \(P_\mathrm{obs}\): bias, RSD, Alcock–Paczyński, shot noise, redshift errors
  • Nuisance: \(\ln[b\sigma_8]\) and \(P_s\) in each bin
Cosmological parameters: Ωb, Ωm, h, ns, σ8, w0, wa. Source: Casas et al., arXiv:2303.09451, eqs. 8 and 10, tables 1–3.
Derivatives: step sizes matter
  • CosmicFish: first derivatives of the observables, step 1% of the fiducial value (2N evaluations); MontePython/Fisher: second derivatives of ln L (2N² evaluations), step \(x\,\sigma_\mathrm{marg}\) with \(x=0.1\) (photo) or 0.05 (spectro)
  • Too small a step: solver noise. Too large: non-Gaussian terms, \(F(x)\propto x^2\) (figure)
  • Fisher needs more solver accuracy than MCMC: CAMB Halofit tolerance \(10^{-6}\) instead of \(10^{-3}\), CLASS high precision; with default precision errors are off by up to 11% or more
  • Neutrino paper: 10% step for Σmν; 1% is dominated by solver noise

Take-home
  • Covariance-tuned Metropolis is the workhorse; ensemble samplers need no tuning; HMC needs gradients; nested sampling gives the evidence
  • Fisher: add, fix, marginalise, reparametrise; quote marginal errors
  • A Fisher forecast is only as good as its derivatives: step size and solver precision
  • It fails at prior boundaries and for non-Gaussian posteriors: check with MCMC
  • A likelihood is a function: cloelike plugs into any sampler
  • Check convergence; state your priors
